Use this reference when a review needs consistent severity labels, confidence framing, or pass/fail judgement across teammates in a shared agent.

Do not use severity to dramatise the issue. Use it to decide how much the draft must change before it is safe to send, share, or escalate.

## Severity Levels

### High severity

Use `high` when the issue could materially mislead a customer, trigger an unsafe commitment, damage trust, send work to the wrong owner, or cause an escalation to proceed on weak or false grounds.

Common high-severity findings:

- The draft promises a fix, date, refund, credit, exception, policy decision, or engineering priority without approved evidence.
- The draft states root cause as confirmed when the evidence only supports a hypothesis.
- The draft omits a known high-impact risk, such as data loss, security/privacy sensitivity, broad customer impact, outage-like symptoms, or repeated business-critical failures.
- The draft uses unsupported ticket counts, trend claims, SLA risk, customer impact, or account risk to justify escalation or reporting.
- The draft exposes internal-only reasoning in customer-facing wording.
- The draft gives next steps that are unsafe, impossible, ownerless, or likely to make the customer situation worse.

High-severity finding style:

- State the specific risk.
- Tie it to the exact claim or wording.
- Recommend a safer replacement or route away.
- Mark the artefact as not safe to share as-is unless the high-severity issue is fixed.

### Medium severity

Use `medium` when the issue reduces clarity, confidence, actionability, or evidence quality, but does not by itself create a major customer, operational, or escalation risk.

Common medium-severity findings:

- The draft has partial evidence but the claim is too broad.
- The draft blends confirmed facts with interpretation, but the likely meaning is still recoverable.
- The next step is useful but missing owner, expected decision, or required evidence.
- The draft says “we are investigating” but does not explain what support needs from the customer or next owner.
- A knowledge draft has a plausible workaround but lacks limits, scope, or confirmation that it is reusable.
- A backlog summary includes counts but does not state filters, time window, or confidence level.

Medium-severity finding style:

- Narrow or qualify the claim.
- Add owner/action/detail to next steps.
- Ask for one missing evidence item if needed.
- Usually mark the artefact as mostly ready with fixes, not fully blocked.

### Low severity

Use `low` when the issue is mainly wording, structure, formatting, duplication, or minor ambiguity that does not change the substance or safety of the artefact.

Common low-severity findings:

- The draft has repetitive phrasing.
- The tone is slightly too vague, defensive, or wordy.
- The evidence is present but could be referenced more cleanly.
- The next step is clear enough but could be more concise.
- The pass/fail checklist would benefit from more direct wording.

Low-severity finding style:

- Suggest a concise edit.
- Do not over-route or block sharing only because of low-severity issues.
- Mark as ready or mostly ready depending on whether any higher-severity issues remain.

## Confidence Labels

Use confidence labels only when they help prevent overclaiming.

### Confirmed

Use when the fact is directly supported by supplied Zendesk evidence, pasted ticket content, accessible records, or explicitly supplied source material.

Example:

- “Confirmed: the customer reported the checkout error in the ticket thread.”

### Reported

Use when the information comes from a customer, teammate, or draft but is not independently verified.

Example:

- “Reported: the customer says the issue affects all users in their account.”

### Likely

Use when evidence supports the interpretation, but proof is incomplete.

Example:

- “Likely: the issue is related to the recent configuration change, but the logs have not been reviewed.”

### Possible

Use when the explanation is plausible but weakly supported.

Example:

- “Possible: this may be related to browser caching, but the current evidence is not enough to say.”

### Needs verification

Use when a claim should not be included as fact until the missing evidence is collected.

Example:

- “Needs verification: whether engineering has accepted ownership.”

## Verdict Calibration

### Ready

Use when:

- No high-severity issues remain.
- Medium-severity issues are absent or minor enough not to block sharing.
- The main claims are supported.
- Facts and interpretations are separated.
- Next steps are specific enough for the intended audience.

### Mostly ready with fixes

Use when:

- No unresolved high-severity issue remains, or a high-severity issue has a clear small edit that must be made first.
- One or more medium-severity issues need correction.
- The artefact is directionally safe but should not be sent exactly as written.

### Not ready

Use when:

- A high-severity issue remains unresolved.
- There is no reviewable artefact.
- The evidence is too thin to judge the main claims.
- The draft makes unsupported commitments, root-cause claims, impact claims, escalation claims, or public knowledge claims.
- The safest next step is evidence collection, readiness check, drafting, handoff prep, or escalation prep instead of QA.

## Pass/Fail Checklist Calibration

Use this mapping when answering the final checklist.

- Evidence supports the main claims: `Yes` only when each important claim is supported or clearly labelled as reported/likely/possible/needs verification.
- Facts and interpretation are clearly separated: `Yes` only when confirmed facts cannot be confused with hypotheses or customer reports.
- Next steps are specific and owned: `Yes` only when the next action, owner or owning team, and needed evidence/decision are clear.
- Risk or escalation gaps remain: `Yes` when any high-severity issue remains or when medium-severity risk context is still missing.
- Safe to send or share as-is: `Yes` only when no blocking wording, evidence, ownership, or risk issue remains.

## Escalation of Review Findings

If a review finds a high-severity problem but the user asked only for light wording help, still flag the high-severity issue. Do not silently polish unsafe content.

If a review finds multiple high-severity problems caused by missing evidence, stop after the most important blockers and route to the smallest upstream workflow rather than producing a long speculative critique.
